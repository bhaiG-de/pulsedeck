# DJing — the art

Make the next musical moment worth hearing. Choose the record for the moment, the passages for their relationship, and the technique for those passages. A technically successful handover is only one part of a successful mix.

This skill teaches musicianship from Phil Morse's *Rock the Dancefloor* and Frank Broughton and Bill Brewster's *How to DJ Right*. The references are original practical syntheses; [sources](references/sources.md) identifies the chapters and qualifications. The user's brief determines the artistic goal, including deliberate cuts, unusual pairings, loops, effects and contrasts.

## Make the musical decision

During performance, keep a short working plan; do not narrate a checklist on every transition. Prepare the next viable choice early enough to leave time for a better alternative. When learning, planning or reviewing, explain the decisions explicitly.

1. **Give the next record a job.** Continue a groove, deepen it, raise intensity, create a release, introduce a new color, or close. Name the musical link or intended contrast. For a flowing mixed set, first seek a suitable unplayed record near the current tempo that supports an audible beatmix. Build range through texture, arrangement and coherent stretches of music as well as occasional tempo changes; do not make every new color a large tempo reset. BPM, key and a suggestion score help find candidates; they do not supply this reason. Respect a requested order or collection. Use [selection and energy](references/selection-and-energy.md) for programming, repeat history and recovery choices.
2. **Decide what the current record should finish.** Identify the worthwhile statement: a verse and answer, refrain, developing groove, or buildup and its payoff. Choose an exit after that statement, unless a deliberate interruption serves the brief. File end, time played and the first kick of a drop do not tell you that its job is complete.
3. **Place the incoming arrival, then work backward.** Decide what B should contribute when A gives way. Find B's complete entry and all important changes between entry and arrival. Match their remaining lengths to A's chosen handover. First identify the original phrase origin and the current position within it: at the start of bar k of an N-bar phrase, only `N − (k − 1)` bars remain. Do not reset a partly played phrase's origin to now. Distinguish a beat, the first beat of a bar, and the beginning of the relevant phrase. A bar downbeat is not automatically a phrase start. For phrase placement, unfamiliar arrangements or custom blends, read [phrasing and arrangement](references/phrasing-and-arrangement.md).
4. **Make the overlap a plausible arrangement.** Decide when B first becomes audible, when bass transfers, when its main idea leads, and when A clears. These moments can differ. Give competing vocals, basslines and leads space; keep enough rhythm and body for the intended energy. Use [mixing and blending](references/mixing-and-blending.md) and [transition design](references/transition-design.md) when designing the exchange.
5. **Establish the pulse and pitch as played.** Before arming a rhythmic overlap, calculate each track's change from its own natural local tempo, including inherited rate and Sync behavior. Prefer small changes that preserve the record's character; matching a display does not justify a large stretch. Morse's roughly ±4% is a starting guideline for ordinary beatmixes, not an inaudibility guarantee. If a record needs too much correction, search for a musically suitable closer-tempo alternative before falling back to a reset. Do not assume a wider bend sounds acceptable without listening evidence. Then match local tempo, beat phase and bar identity, and confirm the intended arrivals still coincide. Close natural BPMs alone are not beatmatching; Sync cannot choose the phrase. A large reset needs a musical purpose or an honest recovery reason. Check groove and sounding harmony separately. Read [beatmatching and groove](references/beatmatching-and-groove.md) for rate decisions, gradual changes and timing problems, and [harmony and pitch](references/harmony-and-pitch.md) for tonal overlaps.
6. **Judge the result and carry the lesson forward.** Check actual execution and, where listening or feedback is available, whether the join resolved, the groove stayed convincing, the sound remained balanced, and B delivered the intended next moment. Correct the relevant cause on the next choice. CLI success cannot establish these listening judgments.

For a custom transition, retain enough to execute without guessing:

```text
Purpose / A statement to protect:
A launch → handover → clearance; B entry → desired arrival:
Evidence for those musical boundaries; confidence / unknowns:
Lead intervals and meter; local tempo / Sync / pitch strategy:
First audibility; bass and foreground transfer; internal conflicts:
Next viable alternative if the window or preparation fails:
```

This is a reasoning aid, not a required user-facing form. A straightforward cut needs less planning than a long layered blend. Use exact source coordinates from the library evidence when translating a plan into controls.

For live repairs, use the current advancing positions. Moving a launch earlier is an advance-planning option only while that moment is still in the future. After playback or a loop has already advanced, choose a reachable release, later handover, supported entry change or extension and recount; a past launch cannot repair the present mix.

## Know what the evidence supports

Use [data to decisions](references/data-to-decisions.md) to distinguish heard or human-annotated content from measured timing and texture. Numeric bands cannot tell you a lyric finished, a chorus began, or the room loved a track. With incomplete content evidence, choose passages and exposure that require fewer assumptions; keep the unknowns explicit. Missing cues do not disqualify a song, and a regular beatgrid does not prove its form.

For supported linear custom timelines, [checked phrase plans](references/checked-phrase-plans.md) documents the existing read-only timing helper. It checks coordinates, not musical boundaries or audible quality. Preserve genuine pickups, irregular bars and intended contrasts rather than changing the musical idea just to satisfy its scope.

## Translate judgment into performance

Use [library knowledge](../pulsedeck-library/SKILL.md) for version-specific passages and [deck control](../pulsedeck-control/SKILL.md) for preparation, audition, scheduling and recovery. Choose direct controls, a supported move or an autonomous queue according to the task. The native queue does not read this skill or execute a prose arrangement plan.

For style-specific approaches, consult the [style guide](references/style-and-phrase-manual.md). For concrete counted examples, use [worked cases](references/worked-cases.md). Adapt their relationships to the actual songs. Great selection and placement can call for a simple transition; elaborate mixing needs an audible musical purpose.
