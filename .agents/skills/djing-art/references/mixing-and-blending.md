# Arrange the sound between the records

Book basis: Morse, “Understanding Your Mixer,” “Five Basic DJ Transitions,” and “Adding EQ, Filters And Effects”; Broughton/Brewster chapters 9, 11, 27–29. [Phrase placement](phrasing-and-arrangement.md) chooses the passages; this reference shapes what is audible.

## Give the listener a clear path

Think of an overlap as a temporary arrangement. What useful part of A remains? What useful part of B is arriving? A smooth transition needs compatible contributions and a convincing transfer of attention. Two running decks with one muted do not create an audible blend.

Separate five moments, allowing them to coincide where appropriate:

| Moment | Decide |
|---|---|
| B playback entry | What must run now to arrive later? |
| B first audibility | What should the audience hear from B while A leads? |
| Bass transfer | When can B replace the foundation A supplies? |
| Foreground handover | When should B's principal idea lead? |
| A clearance | When has A finished supporting B, before its next conflict? |

A crossfader midpoint does not identify the musical handover. An abrupt bass exchange can sit inside a gradual audible blend. A silent pre-roll followed by an instantaneous whole-channel swap is a cut.

## Assign roles at the important changes

Track foreground, low-end foundation, rhythmic body and top texture. These roles are perceptual; they are not isolated stems or separate EQ bands. Revisit them whenever a voice, bassline, hook or breakdown enters or leaves.

A useful default is one clear foreground and one convincing bass foundation. This leaves room for the incoming record to add motion without burying the outgoing statement. Purposeful layered hooks or double drops are valid when their rhythms, harmony and density work. Choose them deliberately rather than running two full arrangements because both faders can open.

When A's bass disappears, B needs actual suitable low-end material if the goal is continuous weight. Restoring B's low EQ while it plays only hats cannot create a bassline. Conversely, two full basslines and kicks can overload the mix, smear transients or cancel each other. Deliberate low-end subtraction can create anticipation when a known return provides the release.

For vocals, protect sentences, pickups, responses and intelligibility. B's drums may sit under A's refrain; B's new voice can take over when A finishes. If A repeats a short answer in gaps, a call-and-response can work. Treat lyric content as unknown unless heard or reliably annotated. Removing lows does not remove competing singers.

## Use the mixer for those roles

Use channel faders and crossfader to change contribution, trim/gain to establish suitable level, and EQ to rebalance broad frequency areas. Learn the actual curve and routing from control. Fader position alone cannot tell you the resulting loudness.

A bass-swap approach often introduces B with attenuated lows, transfers the low end at a relevant boundary, then clears A. Adapt the attenuation and timing to the pair. Cutting lows also affects the kick, while vocal and bass harmonics span multiple bands. Broad EQ cannot extract a clean acappella or guarantee removal of a conflicting part.

Prefer removing excess to adding indiscriminate boost. Withholding both foundations can deliberately create anticipation when its duration and release are designed; for continuous drive, retain suitable support from one source. Keep boosts within real headroom rather than using gain as a substitute for arrangement. Reset performance treatments on the freed deck so the next entry does not inherit an unintended filter or missing bass.

## Separate tone, contribution and level

Establish a source-tone baseline from representative played material. Keep room/output correction separate from this baseline, and temporary transition coloration separate from both. Opening a filter toward a return is an arrangement gesture; correcting a persistently over-heavy source is a tonal balance decision. Remember which settings should survive the handover.

## Preserve level and headroom

Compare representative passages, including the incoming dense section, rather than matching a quiet intro to A's loud chorus. Watch the summed output as well as individual channels. Two safe channels can still overload when added. A full-level pair may add roughly 3 dB for uncorrelated material or up to 6 dB for identical coherent signals; neither is a fixed correction for a real mix.

Do not transfer analog-meter “0 dB” advice to digital full scale: 0 dBFS is the digital ceiling. Use the system's actual peak/headroom indicators. PulseDeck's programme normalization helps compare whole tracks; local intro/drop contrast remains. Do not keep raising gain during an intentional breakdown and clip when the drop arrives.

The musical question is whether perceived level and weight remain appropriate, not whether both channels have identical numbers. A planned reduction can be expressive. An unexplained collapse in body during a maintain-drive transition is a design defect.

## Effects should complete a musical gesture

Specify the event, target, duration and ending: a brief echo of a completed word, a filter opening toward a known arrival, or a restrained tail joining textures. Respect existing production effects and space for the next foreground. Busy source material may need no additional gesture.

Know the actual signal path through the control skill. Per-channel processing and supported native exit recipes can route tails differently; PulseDeck's native echo/reverb exits expose independent post-fader returns. Do not assume a closed fader either kills or preserves every effect tail. Use the executor's documented send closure and bounded return when choosing that recipe. A loop or effect cannot by itself repair wrong phrase placement, an unfinished lyric or a missing replacement groove. Use the controls whose audible contribution serves the idea.


## Choose from the whole vocabulary by function

| Musical situation | Useful control construction |
|---|---|
| A supports B's sparse approach | Reveal B's useful body/top while A carries lows; exchange lows at B's real return |
| A's foreground is finishing while B's body becomes denser | Reduce A's channel or selected mid/high contribution around completion, then expose B's foreground; broad EQ changes tone and cannot isolate a singer |
| Different persistent source tone or local impact | Establish trim/EQ baseline from representative normalized material and preserve headroom through the later dense passage |
| A clean unit must persist until B arrives | Use a counted, seam-checked loop with a planned release; re-count the source/performance timeline |
| B's attractive arrival has an unsuitable long lead | Use a different complete cue/entry and recount, rather than playing all of the lead behind closed lows |
| A completed motif deserves a tail | Shape a bounded echo/reverb send around its ending, leaving space for B's next foreground |
| A gradual pulse change serves the next section | Place a deliberate source-relative tempo program in suitable material; separate it from phase repair |

Controls are a vocabulary, not a usage quota. A whole-channel exchange may correctly transfer a dense foreground; a bass swap may be enough for complementary arrangements. Choose more controls only when the source map identifies another role to shape. Useful restraint and arbitrary repetition are different: the reason for each retained or changed control must follow this pair's evolving music.
